Why do web agencies look for BugHerd alternatives?

The most common reason agencies start looking for a BugHerd alternative is a variation of the same story: a client emails them about a broken layout or a missing button, and the agency finds out that the bug has been live for days or weeks. BugHerd only catches bugs that clients notice, annotate, and actually submit. Many clients either don't bother reporting small issues, don't know how to use the tool, or simply stop using it after the initial excitement wears off.

Other reasons agencies switch:

  • Too client-dependent: If the client doesn't report it, you don't know about it.
  • No pre-launch value: BugHerd requires a live site and an active client — it can't help during development or staging.
  • No visual regression: After a deployment, BugHerd won't tell you if something changed visually.
  • Browser extension friction: Every client needs to install a browser extension, which creates adoption drop-off.

What does Klavity do differently from BugHerd?

Klavity doesn't wait for anyone to report a bug — it finds bugs itself. Point Klavity at any URL and it runs an AI-powered scan that checks for visual layout issues, broken interactive elements, accessibility violations, and functional regressions. You get a bug report before any human has visited the page.

This changes the workflow fundamentally. Instead of triaging client feedback after the fact, you ship knowing the site is clean. Clients stop being your QA team.

Klavity is especially useful at two moments BugHerd can't help with:

  • Pre-launch: Scan your staging environment before handing off to the client.
  • Post-deploy: Automatically re-scan after every deployment to catch regressions the moment they're introduced.

When should you stick with BugHerd?

BugHerd is genuinely good at what it does — collecting structured client feedback. If your main challenge is organising client bug reports rather than finding bugs in the first place, BugHerd's workflow (kanban board, annotated screenshots, client comments) is hard to beat. It's also useful for content-heavy projects where clients are reviewing copy and design rather than reporting technical issues.

Stick with BugHerd if:

  • Your clients are technically engaged and consistently submit feedback
  • Your QA process is solid and you mainly need a client communication layer
  • You're working on content or design reviews, not complex web apps

They're better than BugHerd in some ways (no extension required, cleaner UI) but they all share the same fundamental model: a human has to find the bug first.
